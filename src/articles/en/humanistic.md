Several schools come under this umbrella: person-centred therapy, Gestalt, existential therapy and others. What unites them is the belief that people have an inner potential for growth and that the main thing a therapist can give is genuine presence and acceptance.

## What a session looks like

A lot of conversation, but no advice. The therapist listens closely, reflects what they hear and asks questions that help you hear yourself. In Gestalt there may be more experimental exercises, such as “talking” to an empty chair where you imagine someone. In existential therapy the topics are often the bigger questions: meaning, freedom, choice, loneliness, mortality.

## When people choose it

When they feel lost, in transition (a breakup, a change of path, a loss), or are looking for a more authentic life. When they do not want a structured programme but a space to be heard.

## What to keep in mind

This approach is usually less aimed at specific symptoms and less “instrumental”. If you have a very specific difficulty, like panic attacks, you can discuss with a professional whether this path is the best fit or whether you need something more targeted.

No approach is best for everyone. What suits you depends on you, on the difficulty and on the person sitting across from you. That is why it is best to talk it through with a psychologist or psychotherapist.
