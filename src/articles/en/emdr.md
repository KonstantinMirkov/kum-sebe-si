EMDR stands for “eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing”. It was developed to work with the aftermath of traumatic experiences. It sounds odd, and at first glance may seem unusual, but it is a recognised method mentioned in World Health Organization and NICE recommendations for people with post-traumatic stress.

## How it goes

The first sessions are preparation: getting acquainted, going over history, learning calming techniques. Then, when you are ready, you work with a specific memory. While you think about it, the therapist guides bilateral stimulation, most often by asking you to follow moving fingers with your eyes. Alternatives are tapping or sounds. Afterwards you describe what you notice. The aim is for the memories to lose their sharp emotional charge, so they feel like the past rather than the present.

## What to know

It is not hypnosis: you stay fully in control and can stop. Sometimes during treatment or after it, experiences can get stronger for a short while, which is why preparation and trust matter. It is not for everyone and not necessary for every trauma.

## What to ask

Whether the therapist has specialised EMDR training, how you will know you are ready, what you do if it gets too heavy.

No approach is best for everyone. What suits you depends on you, on the difficulty and on the person sitting across from you. That is why it is best to talk it through with a psychologist or psychotherapist.
