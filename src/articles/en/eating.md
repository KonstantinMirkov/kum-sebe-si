Eating is not only about food. It is tied to emotions, habits, control, shame, and sometimes to a wish to cope with something that hurts. That is why eating difficulties are rarely “just a diet that went too far”.

## What it can look like

Being preoccupied with calories, weight or body shape. Strict rules about what, when and how much can be eaten. Episodes of eating that you feel you cannot stop, followed by shame and attempts to “make up for it”. Hiding food, avoiding meals with others. Physically: changes in weight, fatigue, dizziness, irregular periods.

Eating difficulties occur in people of every size, age and gender. You cannot spot them by appearance.

## What is important to know

They are not a matter of willpower or vanity. They can be serious for health, but they are treatable and many people recover, especially if help comes earlier. Support usually involves a doctor, a psychotherapist and often a dietitian working together.

## What to do

If you recognise yourself, the first step is to tell someone you trust and see your family doctor. If you are worried about someone close, talk to them calmly and without comments about looks or food. If there is dizziness to the point of fainting, chest pain or a sense of danger to life, call **112**.
